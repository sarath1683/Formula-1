    Chapter 1

    Historical background

    A car is always close to the ground, which makes its airflow harder to understand than an aircraft's. Both fight drag, but where a plane needs lift, a racing car wants the opposite: downforce, which presses the tyres into the track so they can accelerate, brake and corner harder.

    Chapter 2

    Out of thin air

    An airliner leaves the ground purely because its wings push on thin air, and an F1 car is often said to make enough downforce to drive on a ceiling. This chapter explains where forces that large come from, using nothing more magical than Newton's laws.

    Ceiling check

    Using the book's 1996 F1 figures (–CL = 2.56, frontal area 1.35 m²) and an assumed 600 kg car with driver, downforce equals weight at about 190 km/h. Above that speed, the car could in principle hold itself to the ceiling.

    V = √(2mg ÷ (ρ · CL · A)). Getting it up there is left as an exercise.

    Part 1 · The sources of the forces

    Newton's three laws, applied to air

    Air has mass, so everything Newton said about solid objects applies to it. Stick your hand out of a car window and tilt it: you feel drag pushing it back, and lift pushing it up or down.

    1

    Inertia

    A body stays at rest, or keeps moving at the same speed and direction, unless a force acts on it. To turn the airflow round a wing, something must push on the air.

    2

    F = m · a

    The force needed for a given acceleration is proportional to mass. A heavier car needs more force to speed up, slow down or corner. That reluctance is inertia.

    3

    Action and reaction

    Every force has an equal and opposite reaction. A wing pulls the air towards it, so the air pulls the wing the other way. That reaction is downforce.

    Forces are drawn as vectors: the arrow points the way the force acts, and its length shows how big it is. Pick a situation to see the book's Figures 2-1 to 2-3.

    Part 2 · Air properties

    How much air does a car disturb?

    As a car passes, it accelerates and deflects the air around it. The mass of air it disturbs, and so the size of the reaction forces, depends on three things:

    Size of the car

    A bigger car (and its shape) disturbs more air, so it feels bigger forces at the same speed.

    Density of the air

    Mass per unit volume, 1.225 kg/m³ in the book. Set by temperature and pressure, so we can't change it.

    Speed through the air

    The faster the car, the more it disturbs the air. Forces grow with speed squared.

    Treating air as incompressible

    Air is a gas and can be squeezed, but at racing speeds its density barely changes, so aerodynamicists treat density as constant. Weather and altitude cause small changes only.

    Beyond the book The usual rule is that compressibility only matters above about Mach 0.3, roughly 370 km/h at sea level. Even at 340 km/h the density change around a car is only about 4%.

    Relative motion

    A car moving through still air is treated the same as a still car with air moving over it. That is what makes wind tunnels work.

    Part 3 · Viscosity

    Stickiness and the boundary layer

    Viscosity is a fluid's stickiness. It isn't density: oil is more viscous than water yet floats on it. Air flows past a body in thin layers. The layer touching the surface sticks to it and doesn't move. Each layer above is held back a little by the slower one beneath, so they slide over each other until, far enough out, the air moves at the full freestream speed.

    The book's Figure 2-4: velocity grows from zero at the surface to freestream at the edge of the boundary layer.

    Shear forces

    Where layers slide past each other, viscosity creates shear forces between them, a bit like friction between solids. The key point is that viscosity steals kinetic energy from the air near the surface and turns it into heat. The heat is tiny, but the lost energy matters a lot, because tired air struggles to stay attached later on.

    τ = μ · (dV / dy)

    Shear stress τ grows with viscosity μ and with how steeply velocity changes away from the surface. Not in the book, but it is the standard way to write this.

    How the boundary layer grows along a car

    Viscous losses build up with distance, so the boundary layer thickens towards the rear. It starts laminar and a few millimetres thick at the nose, then turns turbulent and grows to perhaps a few centimetres at the back. The drawing exaggerates the thickness so you can see it.

    Blue: thin laminar layer. Amber: thicker turbulent layer. Thicker boundary layers mean more drag.

    Part 4 · Laminar and turbulent flow

    Smooth layers or swirling mix

    Blow out a candle: smoke rises in straight, laminar streamlines, then breaks into turbulent swirls.

    The difference

    Laminar: all particles move in the average direction and their streamlines stay parallel.

    Turbulent: particles take erratic, crossing paths, even though the average speed and direction can be the same.

    What causes transition

    • Distance: viscous effects keep draining energy until the layers start mixing
    • A change of surface curvature
    • Surface roughness, such as seams, rivets or dirt
    • Higher speed and longer bodies, which together mean a higher Reynolds number (Part 9)

    Even a perfectly smooth surface can't keep the flow laminar forever. As viscosity removes energy, the small velocity differences between sub-layers grow, the layers start to interact, and the flow becomes random. Turbulent layers grow thicker, faster, because of their sideways velocity components.

    The book's Figure 2-5 over a smooth flat plate: thin laminar layer, transition, then a thicker turbulent layer.

    Part 5 · From Aristotle to Bernoulli

    Where the flow is fastest, the pressure is least

    Ps + ½ρV² = constant = total pressure

    Two forms of energy

    Ps, static pressure: pressure energy, the air's ability to push on surfaces.

    ½ρV², dynamic pressure: kinetic energy of a unit volume of air (½mV², with density standing in for mass).

    Energy can't be created or destroyed, only converted. So if the air speeds up, its static pressure must drop.

    When it is valid

    • Only along one streamline (one mean flow path)
    • No viscosity and no energy losses
    • Incompressible flow
    • Used to compare two points, so the constant's value doesn't matter

    Bernoulli around an inverted wing

    Tap a point on the wing to see what Bernoulli says happens there.

    Like the book's Figure 2-8: pressure coefficient along the chord. By convention, negative (suction) is plotted upwards. The shaded gap between the curves is the downforce.

    Lower (suction) surface

    Air speeds up round the leading edge to a peak a short way back, where pressure is lowest. Then it slows and pressure rises again towards ambient at the trailing edge. That rising part is the pressure recovery region. Most of the downforce comes from this surface.

    Upper (pressure) surface

    Air is slower here, so pressure stays slightly above ambient along most of the chord. It adds some downforce, but less than the suction underneath.

    Part 6 · Conservation of mass

    The venturi tube

    The mass of air passing each section per second must be the same, because air can't pile up or vanish. Squeeze the area and the air must speed up, and by Bernoulli its pressure drops. A carburettor uses that low pressure in the throat to suck fuel through its jet. The diverging part after the throat is a diffuser: it slows the air again and recovers the pressure.

    ρ·V₁·A₁ = ρ·V₂·A₂ = ρ·V₃·A₃

    Same idea as the floor and diffuser under a ground-effect car (Chapter 6).

    Part 7 · Turning the flow

    Why air follows a curved surface

    Two forces do the turning

    1. At the surface: the air's adhesive stickiness (viscosity) holds the layer touching the surface, so it follows the curve. Without a force, Newton's first law says the air would carry straight on past the lowest point.

    2. Further away: the air turning sharply next to the surface speeds up, so static pressure there drops most. That low pressure pulls the air further out round the curve too.

    The result: by Newton's third law, pulling the air towards the wing pulls the wing towards the air. On an inverted wing, that is downforce.

    Like Figure 2-7: pressure falls towards the curved surface, pulling outer streamlines round. The reaction on the wing is downforce.

    Try it: the paper experiment

    Hold a sheet of A4 paper by one short edge just under your lower lip so it droops, then blow across its top. The paper rises into the airstream, a force at right angles to the flow. The book calls this the Coanda effect. It isn't the source of lift on wings, but it shows air flowing over a curve can create a force.

    The influence reaches well beyond the surface

    The pressure changes around a body don't stop at its skin; they spread a long way into the air. You can feel it when you're passed by a lorry on the motorway. In racing it means a car affects the cars ahead of, beside and behind it, which is why a following car loses front downforce when it gets close (Chapter 1).

    Each car carries a field of altered pressure. The following car's front wing sits in slower, disturbed air from the leader's wake.

    Part 8 · Pressure gradients

    Downhill, uphill, and letting go

    Air rolls happily from high to low pressure (favourable gradient), but climbing from low back to high pressure (unfavourable) needs energy.

    Two kinds of gradient

    Favourable: high pressure to low, like a ball rolling downhill. From the leading edge to the suction peak.

    Unfavourable (adverse): low pressure to high, uphill. From the suction peak to the trailing edge. The air only makes it to the top if it has enough kinetic energy and the slope isn't too steep.

    If it can't, the flow separates from the surface. Total separation over a wing is a stall.

    Separation lab

    Increase the inverted wing's angle. Past a point, the air on the suction side can't climb the pressure hill and separates. Under the separated region the air near the surface reverses into a recirculating separation bubble.

    Causes of separation: too steep an angle, too low a speed, a steep adverse gradient, a tired (thick, low-energy) boundary layer, or a sharp lip or sudden change of shape.

    The wake, and the rear-wiper puzzle

    The same thing happens at the back of a road car's roof: fast flow slows down, the boundary layer has thickened, and at a sharp lip or steep drop the flow separates into the highly disturbed region called the wake (the book's Figure 2-9).

    Blunt rear: the flow separates at the roof edge and the wake recirculates against the rear glass.

    Sloping rear: the flow stays attached down the rear window and separates later, at the boot edge.

    Beyond the book That is why SUVs and hatchbacks need a rear wiper and saloons usually don't. On a blunt rear, the recirculating wake carries road spray and dirt forwards onto the glass. On a saloon, attached flow keeps the rear window swept clean.

    Part 9 · Turbulence isn't always bad

    Re-energising the boundary layer

    A laminar boundary layer has less skin friction, so less drag. But a turbulent layer can resist separation better, and can even make separated flow reattach. Its sideways velocity components stir faster air from outside down towards the surface, bringing in fresh energy.

    Vortex generators are small fins on a surface. Each one sheds a little swirling vortex that mixes fast outer air into the tired boundary layer, so the flow stays attached further back. Laminar isn't always the goal.

    Without (top): the tired boundary layer separates early. With vortex generators (bottom): swirls mix in energy and the flow stays attached.

    Part 10 · Scale models

    Reynolds number

    A half-scale model in a wind tunnel doesn't behave like the real car at the same speed, because its boundary layer has had half the distance to grow. Separation tends to happen further back on the model. The Reynolds number tells you when flows over different-sized bodies will look alike: match it, and the flow patterns match.

    Re = ρ·V·L ÷ μ  ≈ 67,778 × V × L

    V in m/s and L in metres (imperial: Re ≈ 6,300 × V × L with ft/s and ft). Re has no units.

    Figure 2-10: at the same speed, the boundary layer at the end of a plate of length L/2 is thinner than at the end of length L.

    That is why F1 moved to larger models tested at higher speeds. In 1998 Benetton tried something cleverer: pressurise the tunnel to twice atmospheric pressure. That doubles the air density, so a half-scale model at the same speed matches full-size Re. Technical problems stopped it being used, but the idea was sound.

    Part 12 · The forces involved

    Lift, drag, and the coefficients

    Figure 2-11: drag and lift (here downforce) add as vectors into one resultant aerodynamic force.

    Pressure difference = Cp × q, and force = pressure × area. Adding up all the local pressures over a body gives an overall coefficient for each direction:

    Lift = CL · ½ρV² · A
    Drag = CD · ½ρV² · A

    For cars, A is the frontal area; for wings it is the plan area.

    Side forces matter for stability, but they come from gusty crosswinds and nearby cars. Because they are brief and unpredictable, they are hard to model.

    Types of drag

    Form (pressure) drag

    High pressure on forward-facing surfaces plus low pressure behind separated flow at the rear. The biggest source for most cars.

    Skin friction (viscous) drag

    Viscous air rubbing on the surface and its roughness. Small on most competition cars.

    Profile drag

    Form drag plus skin friction, the usual way to describe a wing's non-lift drag.

    Vortex (induced) drag

    Any body making lift or downforce sheds swirling vortices with low-pressure cores. They pull on rearward-facing surfaces. Big on cars with large wings.

    Main causes of drag on a racing car, roughly from most to least

    1. The basic shape of the car
    2. Wheels and wheel housings
    3. External devices such as wings and spoilers
    4. Internal flows for cooling and ventilation
    5. Imperfections: door handles, mirrors, window seals, panel gaps

    How big are the coefficients?

    Figure 2-12: a flat plate across the flow has CD above 1.1. A teardrop four to five times longer than it is wide has about 0.04.

    The GTR has the bigger frontal area but makes less drag, so its shape must be cleaner: covered wheels and fewer downforce devices.     Aerodynamic efficiency, –L/D

    Downforce divided by drag. The F1 car's 3.0 against the GTR's 0.6 suggests it is five times as efficient. McBeath warns that this ignores each car's purpose and rules, so –L/D is best used to compare cars within one category, or one team's cars over time.

    Where the numbers come from

    Wind tunnel: the car or model sits on a balance that measures drag and downforce directly at a known airspeed. Dividing the force by q × A gives CD and CL.

    CFD: the software works out pressure and shear on every patch of the surface. Adding them up gives the total forces, then the coefficients the same way.

    Without either: measure frontal area and top speed and work CD backwards from the power equation below, or use the straight-line tests in Chapter 8.

    The tunnel and CFD descriptions are general background; the book covers them in detail in Chapters 3 and 8.

    Part 13 · Drag and power

    Power absorbed by drag

    bhp = CD · A · V³ ÷ 146,600  (mph, ft²)
    bhp = CD · A · V³ ÷ 1,225  (m/s, m²)

    Drag grows with V², and power is force × speed, so the power drag absorbs grows with V³. Going 10% faster takes about 33% more power. Multiply bhp by 0.746 for kW.

    Use available power at the wheels, not flywheel power. The book's correction factors: ×0.91 for rear-engined single-seaters on narrow or cold tyres, ×0.875 for circuit single-seaters on hot wide tyres (F1, F3), ×0.85 for race saloons and sports cars, ×0.82 for front-engined rear-drive cars.

    Like Figure 2-14: where each drag-power curve meets the car's available power is its theoretical top speed. F1: 700 bhp × 0.875 = 612.5. GTR: 600 × 0.85 = 510.

    Top speed rarely decides lap time, except on ovals and perhaps at Le Mans. But knowing CD helps work out how much extra drag you can afford for more downforce (Chapter 5).

    Part 14 · Downforce, grip and balance

    It's all about friction

    F = μR. Twice the normal force R gives twice the friction. Downforce adds to R without adding mass.

    F = μ · R

    μ is the coefficient of friction, set by the two surfaces: tiny for an ice-hockey puck on wet ice, huge for a Top Fuel dragster's tyres on sticky asphalt. R is the normal force pressing them together, usually the weight.

    Contact area isn't in the equation. A complication: real tyres' μ drops slightly as R rises, but total grip still increases.

    So why are racing tyres wide?

    If area doesn't appear in F = μR, width shouldn't matter. McBeath says width is about matching the tyre's thermal and mechanical behaviour to the car and track, so it runs at the right temperature for grip.

    Beyond the book Rubber isn't a simple solid. It grips partly by keying into the road surface, and its μ falls as the load per unit area rises. A wider tyre spreads the same load over more rubber, so each bit works at a lower load, where μ is higher. It also has more rubber to share the heat.

    Cornering lab: the book's worked example

    In a steady corner, the maximum friction exactly supplies the centripetal force: μR = mV²/r, so Vmax = √(μRr ÷ m). With downforce, R = mg + downforce, and downforce rises with V², so each speed gain brings yet more grip.

    Defaults are the book's: 500 kg, μ 1.4, 90° corner of 50 m radius, 1996 F1 –CL 2.56 × 1.35 m².

    Part 15 · Production cars and lift

    Some saloons are upside-down wings

    Figure 2-15: a smooth modern saloon profile over a lifting aerofoil. Fast, smooth flow over the roof means low pressure on top, so the car makes lift.

    Chasing low CD, road cars got raked windscreens and rounded edges. That keeps the air over the top fast and smooth, which is exactly what a wing does to make lift.

    Numbers: a road saloon might make up to +82 kg of lift at 160 km/h. Its touring-car version, with airdam, splitter and wing, makes about 50 kg of downforce. That is 132 kg more load on the tyres, about 13% more cornering grip, which is huge where hundredths count.

    Part 16 · Non-Bernoulli downforce?

    The wake and slipstreaming

    The low-pressure wake drags leaves along behind a car and pulls a following car forward.

    In a car's wake the air is slow relative to the car, yet the pressure is low. Simple Bernoulli says slow air should mean high pressure, so something else is going on. The wake has lost energy, turned into heat and sound by separation and viscosity, so its total pressure is lower.

    That low pressure is real. Dust and leaves follow a car along, and you can feel a lorry's suction when you drive up behind it. In racing it lets a following car slipstream: less drag, more straight-line speed.

    McBeath's verdict: it isn't truly "non-Bernoulli". Bernoulli is conservation of energy, and the losses are part of the energy budget.
